**The biggest risk in buying a lot out here isn't overpaying. It's buying land you can't legally build on, can't get water to, or that the "seller" never owned.** This checklist covers the steps we walk every land buyer through: verifying the seller, confirming water and well rules inside the **Pinal Active Management Area**, septic, legal access, earth fissures, zoning and title insurance.

## Step 1: Prove the Seller Is the Real Owner

Vacant land is the property type most often hit by **seller impersonation fraud**. In the **American Land Title Association's 2026 study**, **82% of title firms** named it as a common target. If a fake seller takes your money, getting it back is slow and uncertain.

**Protect yourself:**
- Buy through a **licensed Arizona title and escrow company**, never a direct wire to a seller.
- Be wary of **below-market cash deals with a rushed timeline**.
- Expect your agent and escrow officer to **verify the seller's identity** through a live video call or in person, not just emailed ID.
- Never accept wiring instructions by email without **calling escrow at a number you looked up yourself**.
- Buy an **owner's title insurance policy**. It's your backstop if a forged deed turns up later.

## Step 2: Figure Out Who Controls the Land

Two parcels a mile apart can follow completely different rules.

- **Unincorporated San Tan Valley** falls under **Pinal County** zoning and permits.
- **Queen Creek** has its own town zoning, and the town sits in **both Maricopa and Pinal County**.
- **HOA or CC&Rs:** Some acreage areas have deed restrictions on animals, home size or outbuildings.

**Ask before you offer:** What's the zoning? What's the minimum lot size? Can I put a horse barn, casita or shop on it? Your agent should pull this with you.

## Step 3: Water Is the Deal-Maker (or Deal-Breaker)

Most of this area sits inside the **Pinal AMA**, one of Arizona's five **Active Management Areas** where the **Arizona Department of Water Resources (ADWR)** tightly controls groundwater.

### Option A: A Water Provider Serves the Lot
The easiest path. Get a **written will-serve letter** from the provider and ask about **connection fees** and line extension costs.

### Option B: Drill a Private "Exempt" Well
- An **exempt well** can pump **up to 35 gallons per minute** for household use.
- **Inside an AMA, only one exempt well** is generally allowed for the same use at the same location.
- **The 100-foot rule:** You generally **can't drill a new exempt well** if any part of your land is within **100 feet** of the operating water system of a municipal provider with an **assured water supply designation**.
- You must file a **Notice of Intent to Drill** with ADWR and use a licensed driller.
- **Cost:** Industry estimates for Pinal County put wells at roughly **300 to 700 feet deep** and **about $11,000 to $30,000** all-in. Get real bids.

**Before closing, confirm:** Is there an existing registered well? What does the **ADWR well registry** show for nearby wells, including depth and water level?

## Step 4: Septic and Soils

Raw land here typically needs a **septic system**, which requires county approval and a **percolation (perc) test** or site evaluation. Well and septic placement must meet **setback rules**, so plan them together. On small or oddly shaped lots, setbacks can kill a build.

## Step 5: Legal Access and Easements

A dirt road to the lot doesn't mean you have a **legal right** to use it.
- Confirm **recorded access** (a public road or recorded easement).
- Check **utility easements** that limit where you can build.
- Order a **survey** if the corners aren't clearly marked.

## Step 6: Earth Fissures, Floodplains and Subsidence

Decades of groundwater pumping in central Arizona have caused **land subsidence** and **earth fissures** in parts of Pinal County.
- Check the **Arizona Geological Survey earth fissure maps**.
- Check **FEMA flood maps** and look for washes that run through or near the parcel.
- Walk the land after a monsoon storm if you can.

## Step 7: Beware "Wildcat" Lot Splits

Arizona land that's been **split repeatedly** without going through a formal subdivision process can come with problems: no guaranteed road maintenance, utility gaps or trouble getting permits. Ask how and when the parcel was created.

## Step 8: Know the Money Side

- **Land loans** usually need a **bigger down payment** and have shorter terms than home mortgages. Many land buyers pay cash.
- Budget for **utility extensions, well, septic, driveway, grading and impact fees** on top of the price.
- Check **property taxes** with the county Treasurer and Assessor.

## Raw Land Due Diligence Checklist

1. ☐ Seller identity verified through title and escrow
2. ☐ Owner's title insurance ordered
3. ☐ Zoning and allowed uses confirmed
4. ☐ Water: will-serve letter **or** exempt well eligibility confirmed with ADWR
5. ☐ Septic feasibility checked
6. ☐ Legal access and easements confirmed
7. ☐ Survey ordered
8. ☐ Fissure and flood maps checked
9. ☐ Build budget beyond purchase price
10. ☐ After closing: register for **ERAS** at earlyalert.azre.gov and your **county recorder alert**

*General information only. Confirm water, septic and zoning details with the agencies and licensed pros involved.*

## Key Takeaways

- **Verify the seller first.** Vacant land is the #1 target for impersonation fraud.
- **Water rules are strict** inside the Pinal AMA. Confirm a provider or exempt well eligibility **before** you buy.
- **The 100-foot rule** can block a new exempt well near a municipal water system.
- **Access, septic, fissures and floodplains** can make a "cheap" lot expensive.
- **After closing, sign up for free alerts** so no one can steal your land.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I drill a well on land in San Tan Valley?

Often, yes, but San Tan Valley is inside the Pinal Active Management Area. You can generally drill one exempt well (up to 35 gallons per minute) for domestic use, unless your land is within 100 feet of a designated municipal provider's water system. File a Notice of Intent with ADWR first.

### How much does a well cost in Pinal County?

Industry estimates put Pinal County wells around 300 to 700 feet deep and about $11,000 to $30,000 all-in. Depth and cost vary by location, so get bids from licensed drillers.

### What should I check before buying vacant land in Queen Creek?

Confirm which county and zoning apply, water service or well eligibility, septic feasibility, legal access, easements, flood and fissure risk, and that the seller's identity is verified by title and escrow.

### Is it safe to buy land from an out-of-state seller?

It can be, but it's the exact pattern fraudsters copy. Close through a licensed Arizona title and escrow company, insist on strong identity verification and buy owner's title insurance.

### Do I need title insurance on raw land?

We strongly recommend it. An owner's policy protects you if a forged deed or hidden defect in the chain of title surfaces after you buy.
