**If you own a vacant lot, a paid-off rental or an empty house anywhere in San Tan Valley or Queen Creek, you are exactly who deed thieves are looking for.** This guide explains how seller impersonation fraud works, why empty land in Pinal and Maricopa County is the easiest target, the warning signs we watch for as agents, and the three free Arizona alerts that take about 15 minutes to set up.

## What Is Deed Fraud?

**Deed fraud** (also called **title theft** or **seller impersonation fraud**) happens when a criminal pretends to be the owner of a property and either:

1. **Sells it** to a real buyer and walks away with the cash, or
2. **Records a forged deed** that moves the property into their name, then borrows against it or resells it.

The real owner usually finds out months later, when a tax bill stops arriving, a neighbor mentions construction on "their" lot, or a new owner shows up with a key.

## Why Vacant Land Is the Number One Target

The **American Land Title Association (ALTA)** released its newest Seller Impersonation Fraud study in September 2026. The findings:

- **59% of title companies** reported at least one seller impersonation attempt in 2025, **more than double the 28%** in ALTA's 2024 study.
- **82%** named **vacant land** as a common target, more than any other property type.
- **Vacation homes (51%)**, **rentals (48%)**, **agricultural land (36%)** and even **primary residences (25%)** are now being targeted too.
- About **three out of four** respondents said **absentee owners** are common targets, and **68%** pointed to properties owned **free and clear** with no mortgage.

The reason is simple. A vacant lot has no one living on it to notice. There's often no lender watching the title, and land usually sells for **all cash**, so no mortgage underwriter asks hard questions.

## Why This Hits Close to Home Here

San Tan Valley and the Pinal County side of Queen Creek have exactly what these criminals want:

- **Acreage parcels and horse properties** in unincorporated Pinal County, many held for years by out-of-state owners
- **Investment lots** bought during earlier growth waves and still waiting to be built on
- **Snowbird and second homes** that sit empty for months
- **Inherited properties** where the original owner has passed and the family lives elsewhere

**Queen Creek straddles two counties.** Part of the town sits in **Maricopa County** and part in **Pinal County**, so owners need to know which recorder's office holds their records.

## How the Scam Plays Out, Step by Step

1. **The fraudster picks a parcel** from free public records, often land with no mortgage and a mailing address out of state.
2. **They contact a local agent** by text or email, posing as the owner. They usually say they're traveling or working overseas.
3. **They ask for a fast sale**, often priced **below market** for cash.
4. **They refuse to meet or video call** and want to sign with a **mobile or remote notary they arrange themselves**.
5. **They send a fake ID** and sometimes use real personal details (a birthdate or even a Social Security number) stolen from data breaches.
6. **They ask for proceeds by wire** to an account that doesn't match the owner's name.

The **FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)** logged **12,368 real estate fraud complaints** and **$275.1 million in losses** in 2025, up from about $173 million in 2024.

## Red Flags We Watch For on Every Listing

As a team with **21 years** in this market, we treat these as stop signs:

- The seller **won't meet in person or on live video**
- Urgency plus a **below-market price** on land or a vacant home
- The seller wants to **pick their own notary**
- Contact info **doesn't match** anything in county or assessor records
- A request to **change the payout account** late in the deal
- Vague answers about the property itself (utilities, access, HOA, why they bought it)

If a "seller" fails our verification, we don't list the property. Title companies are tightening checks too, and ALTA reports **94% of firms** now use multiple fraud-detection tools.

## Arizona Just Got Tougher on Deed Fraud

**Senate Bill 1479**, signed April 9, 2026, took effect in September 2026. Key changes:

- **Photo ID to record in person.** Anyone presenting a deed at the county recorder's counter needs a valid, unexpired government photo ID. Escrow, title, banks, attorneys and government agencies are exempt. **Pinal County started enforcing this on September 14, 2026.**
- **Notary thumbprints** in the journal for deeds and other key real estate documents
- **Remote online notarizations** must keep the video for at least **seven years**
- **Recording a forged deed** is now a **felony**, not a misdemeanor
- **The five-year loophole is gone.** A rule that could let an uncontested forged deed stand after five years (at issue in *Dominguez v. Dominguez*) was repealed.

The new law helps, but it doesn't replace watching your own property.

## 3 Free Alerts Every Local Owner Should Set Up

1. **Arizona Department of Real Estate Early Real Estate Sales Alert System (ERAS).** Register at **earlyalert.azre.gov** by name, address or parcel number (APN). You get a text and email when an escrow officer enters a **pending sale** on your property, **before closing**.
2. **Your County Recorder's alert.** **Pinal County Recording Notification** or **Maricopa Title Alert** emails you when a document is **recorded** under the name you monitor.
3. **Pinal County Assessor Parcel Alert List (PAL).** Sign up **in person** with ID. The Assessor contacts you when it receives a **new deed** or a **mailing address change** on your parcel.

**Pro tip:** Sign up under every name on title, including your **trust** or **LLC** name.

## What to Do If You Think Your Property Was Targeted

1. **Call the escrow or title company** on any pending sale right away.
2. **Report it** to your local police or sheriff, the **Arizona Attorney General's Office**, and the FBI at **ic3.gov**.
3. **Contact your title insurer** if you have an owner's policy.
4. **Talk to a real estate attorney** about a **quiet title action**.
5. **Keep every document**, including texts, emails and county notices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is deed fraud common in San Tan Valley?

Deed fraud is still rare compared with the number of sales, but it's rising fast nationally. Title firms reporting at least one attempt jumped from 28% to 59% between ALTA's 2024 and 2026 studies. Vacant land and absentee-owned homes, both common in San Tan Valley, face the highest risk.

### Why do scammers target vacant land?

Vacant land has no occupant to notice a sale, usually no mortgage lender watching the title, and it often sells for all cash with less scrutiny. ALTA's 2026 study found 82% of title firms named vacant land as a common target.

### How can I tell if someone is trying to sell my land?

Register for the Arizona Department of Real Estate's free ERAS alert at earlyalert.azre.gov. It notifies you when a pending sale is entered on your property, before closing.

### Is deed fraud a felony in Arizona?

Yes. Under SB 1479, knowingly recording a forged or false document against real property is now a felony. Owners can also sue for at least $5,000 or triple their damages, plus attorney fees.

### Does the new photo ID rule stop deed fraud?

It closes one gap. Since September 2026, anyone recording a deed in person must show photo ID. It doesn't stop fraud that runs through a real escrow with fake documents, which is why owner alerts still matter.
